callsign-record / anatomy

The parts of a UK amateur callsign, which characters belong in one, and who decides what is allowed – background for the notes shown across the record. This is not official guidance; for that, see Ofcom. Each fact below carries its source and how confident the reading is.

How to read the sources – authoritative means stated in an Ofcom-published document; best available means a reputable secondary source where the primary is silent; institutional knowledge means widely known but not sourced here.
a callsign, taken apart

The diagram below spreads one representative callsign, MW0ABC/P, out with extra spacing and colour-groups each part. Colour is only a secondary cue – every part is labelled in words on the diagram and named again in the key table, so it reads with no colour, no SVG and no scripts. The simpler M7TEE has just a prefix, a digit and a suffix (no Regional Secondary Locator and no operating suffix). The prose that follows explains each part in full.

the parts of a callsign

A UK amateur callsign is a short identifier for one licence, made of capital letters and digits with no spaces. It has three core parts:

prefix
the opening character(s); for the UK these begin G, M or 2.
digit
a single number.
suffix
the ending letters (for example the TEE in an M7TEE-shaped call). This is the sense of “suffix” used across the record.

A callsign may also carry a Regional Secondary Locator (RSL) – a letter inserted after the first character to show which UK nation or Crown Dependency the station is in. Ofcom defines it as “a letter, that is inserted after the initial character of the call sign … to convey the location of the Radio Equipment”. Authoritative – Ofcom, Amateur Radio Wireless Telegraphy Licence Conditions Booklet (OFW611, 21 February 2024), definitions, cl. (w).

Two different meanings of “suffix”. On this record and among amateurs, the suffix is the callsign’s ending letters (TEE in M7TEE) – the ITU/amateur-standard sense, and the one the record always means by “suffix”. Ofcom’s licence T&Cs (cl. 26) use the same word, suffix, for something different: the optional addition after a slash (such as /P). To keep the two apart, this page calls that one the operating suffix (also “post-slash suffix”). The two are unrelated.

How many letters? The suffix is normally three letters. Some very old callsigns – and any starting G2 – carry only two, and such a form is available only to a licensee who previously held it. Ofcom also grants special contest callsigns, whose core is a prefix letter, a digit and a single suffix letter (for example G8Z or M7R), by Notice of Variation. Authoritative – Ofcom, Amateur radio guidance (updated 14 October 2025), §5.2 (normally three letters; the two-letter and G2 legacy forms); §6.4 (special contest callsigns).

The special contest callsign in full. Ofcom's own guidance gives the shape as “(G)(#)(&)(A-Z) and (M)(#)(&)(A-Z), where ‘#’ is an applicable Regional Secondary Locator (if any) and ‘&’ is a digit from 0 to 9” – so the RSL sits in its usual slot, corroborated by the RSGB Contest Committee's own examples GM8C and MW7D – and states that 520 call signs have been made available in total. Variations are granted by Ofcom; the RSGB administers and distributes them and advises Ofcom on applications, though its advice is only a recommendation and the final decision remains Ofcom's. A special contest callsign may only be used in amateur radio contests of no more than 48 hours run by a bona fide contest organiser, and the current cycle of grants is valid until 31 December 2029 – renewable for a further five years if the licensee still qualifies. Applicants must also meet separate qualifying criteria on contest-entry history and achievement, not restated here. Authoritative – Ofcom guidance, as published on the RSGB Special Contest Call Sign application page (captured 2026-07-23).

Clubs have been able to apply for a special contest callsign on simple application since about 1995; individual Full licensees became eligible from 2010, subject to the same contest-experience conditions clubs now also meet. Every special contest callsign's Notice of Variation expires at the end of the same five-year period regardless of when it was granted, and a callsign not renewed is withdrawn for two years before a new applicant may take it over. Best available (RSGB) – RSGB Contest Committee, Special Contest Calls (rsgbcc.org, page states “Updated 20 July 2026”; captured 2026-07-23).

Observed in the register as held, among the core callsigns the record’s parser decomposes into parts: almost all – about 99.7% – carry three trailing letters, and a small remainder – about 0.3% – carry two, overwhelmingly the heritage G-series forms. Forms the parser sets aside are counted separately, not folded in – the visitor/reciprocal slash callsigns and rows that do not fit the standard shape. Some of those carry other lengths, for example the reciprocal M/KQ4U (Reserved), a single trailing letter, and the non-standard 2IFJG (Allocated), four – which the record flags rather than adjudicates. Observation of what the record holds, within the scope stated – derived from the newest held register snapshot (23 June 2026) with this record’s own callsign parser; a data-validity test recomputes the distribution from that snapshot and holds these figures to it.

The near-absence of single-letter suffixes from the held register is expected, not anomalous: a special contest callsign is issued by a Notice of Variation associated with a normal Full licence, not as its own register entry, so this population is witnessed on NoVs rather than in the register. The RSGB Contest Committee's own allocation table declares a per-slot status – Issued, Withdrawn or Available – across the full ~520-slot space (for example G0A → GW4SKA, Issued), making it a declared-availability source the Ofcom register itself never provides for callsigns generally. Best available (RSGB) – RSGB Contest Committee, Special Contest Calls (rsgbcc.org, page states “Updated 20 July 2026”; captured 2026-07-23). Observed / reference – the same per-slot table is held in-repo at reference-data/rsgb-special-contest-calls.csv, refreshed monthly by the scc-sweep workflow (#693).

regional secondary locators

Where an RSL is used it must be one of the letters below. There are two sets: a letter available to all licences, and – the point most often missed – an additional letter available only to a Full (Club) Licence. Authoritative – Ofcom OFW611, cl. 24 (pp 7–8).

A Full (Club) callsign must itself carry an RSL. A club may use one of the all-licences letters – but not E for England – or a letter from a set reserved for club stations. That club-only set supplies an England letter, X, giving the familiar GX/MX club forms (for example GX3DEF). Authoritative – Ofcom, Policy on temporary call signs and call sign enhancement (March 2018), §3.6; the club-only letters are also listed in Ofcom, Amateur radio guidance, §5.7, Table 2.

Using an RSL is normally optional; but for an Intermediate callsign beginning with 2, an RSL must be inserted as the new second character. Ofcom may also notify a licensee of a different character to use “in place of an RSL”. Authoritative – Ofcom OFW611, cl. 22, 23 and 25 (pp 7–8).

special-event and temporary callsigns

To mark a national occasion, Ofcom may notify a temporary RSL – a special letter used in the RSL position, in place of the normal nation letter, for a bounded period of no more than one year. The permission is notified via the Ofcom website; a royal coronation is named among the occasions it covers. Authoritative – Ofcom, Amateur radio guidance (updated 14 October 2025), §5.7.1 (the mechanism, the one-year bound, the “Q” 2022 example, and the royal-coronation occasion); the second-character mechanism and the earlier grants are set out in Ofcom, Policy on temporary call signs and call sign enhancement (March 2018), §5.

A temporary RSL is distinct from a special-event station callsign. A Full licensee (personal or club) may be granted an additional, GB-prefixed callsign by Notice of Variation – for a defined event, or as a permanent showcase station such as GB0IWM for the Imperial War Museum. These GB-prefixed callsigns appear in the register data as a distinct population. Authoritative – Ofcom, Amateur radio guidance, §6.2 (Special Event Station) and §6.3 (Permanent Special Event Station).

licence classes and their prefixes

UK amateur licences come at three classes – Foundation, Intermediate and Full – earned by successive exams; there are also Full (Club) and Full (Temporary Reciprocal) licences. Ofcom’s guidance sets out the callsign formats issued at each class. Authoritative – Ofcom, Licensing guidance document for amateur radio, §5, Table 1 (Amateur Radio Call sign formats).

Attested as published: Ofcom’s Table 1 lists G2 both within the currently-issuing G0–G8 range and as formerly issued. That within-table inconsistency is the source’s own; it is reproduced here as published rather than silently reconciled.

The class scheme has changed over the years, so older or historical records may show legacy categories or callsign formats that are not part of the current three-class scheme. Best available; the detail of past schemes is not researched here. Confidence: low.

A Full (Club) callsign is G or M followed by three letters (two by heritage). In Ofcom’s table the Intermediate format is written 2#0/2#1, where the # stands for the RSL position – the second-character slot an Intermediate 2 callsign must fill. Authoritative – Ofcom guidance, Table 1; the #-as-RSL-slot reading is grounded in OFW611 cl. 25. This record reuses the same # placeholder notation for that slot.

which characters belong

The core callsign uses A–Z and 0–9. After the core, an operating suffix (post-slash suffix – the sense Ofcom’s cl. 26 calls a “suffix”, distinct from the callsign’s ending-letter suffix) may be added after a forward slash /. Ofcom permits any such addition “so long as the station remains identifiable”; the familiar /P (portable), /M (mobile), /MM (maritime mobile), /A and /AM are examples, not a fixed list, and they are “not mandated by, or referenced in, the amateur radio licence”. Authoritative – Ofcom OFW611, cl. 26 (p 8); Ofcom guidance, §5.8 Suffixes.

This “any addition” position is current; the post-slash suffix was previously drawn from a more limited, conventional set. Best available; the earlier history is not dug into here. Confidence: low.

The # you may see is not a letter of the callsign: it marks the RSL slot in Ofcom’s format notation (as in 2#0) and in this record’s own placeholder form. The # notation is optional and style-dependent – some sources use it consistently, some omit it, and some mix the two. The reading here is that “no RSL” is the common default for Foundation and Full holders (especially in England, the largest population), whereas Intermediate callsigns require the RSL letter for everyone, so it appears consistently there. Our reading / best available – not a rigorously published rule. Confidence: low. Anything else – lower-case letters, ordinary spaces, other punctuation, or characters you cannot see – is not part of a normal callsign. Where the published data contains such characters, the record keeps the entry exactly as published and shows the canonical form beside it.

operating away or abroad

A callsign can carry more than one slash. Operating overseas under CEPT Recommendation T/R 61-01, a UK amateur must place “the appropriate country prefix before your call sign” (for example F/M0ABC in France) and may still add an operating suffix – so the transmitted call has both a prefix-slash and a suffix-slash. Authoritative – Ofcom guidance, §7.3 (Your call sign beyond UK jurisdiction).

The mirror image is the visitor prefix – a visitor operating in the UK under a reciprocal arrangement, shown as a UK prefix and RSL before their home callsign (for example MW/ then a non-UK callsign, optionally with /P). These forms appear in the register data. Best available – reciprocal/CEPT operating practice (RSGB, International Prefixes); the exact visitor format is convention, not spelled out in the primary licence documents. Confidence: medium.

Ofcom defines a Full (Temporary Reciprocal) Licence as one issued to a person who holds an amateur licence granted by a recognised foreign administration; it runs for six months, may be renewed once, and Ofcom has stated it will be phased out. The held register data witnesses this Temporary Reciprocal product; the record’s category reference table additionally distinguishes a separate Full (Reciprocal) product, kept apart from it rather than folded together. Authoritative – Ofcom, Statement: Updating the amateur radio licensing framework (11 December 2023), definition (l); Ofcom, Amateur radio guidance, §2.1.4.2.2 (six-month term, renewal, phase-out). Observed / reference – the held snapshots carry “Amateur Temporary Reciprocal Radio Licence”; the two reciprocal products are recorded as distinct in reference-data/licence-category.csv (#232).

who decides what is allowed
The International Telecommunication Union (ITU)
allocates blocks of call-sign series to countries in the Radio Regulations, so a callsign’s opening shows which country issued it. The UK’s amateur prefixes are G, M and 2. Authoritative (instrument) – ITU Radio Regulations, Appendix 42, Table of International Call Sign Series; UK amateur prefixes per Ofcom guidance, Table 1. The full enumerated UK ranges (GAA–GZZ, MAA–MZZ, 2AA–2ZZ, and territory blocks such as VP/VS/ZB/ZN/ZQ) – best available (Wikipedia, Call signs in the United Kingdom); confidence: medium.
Ofcom
the UK regulator – issues the licences and sets the callsign rules. Authoritative – Ofcom OFW611; Ofcom Licensing guidance document for amateur radio; UK Interface Requirement 2028. See the Ofcom amateur radio licensing page.
The Radio Society of Great Britain (RSGB)
administers the amateur exams and publishes operating guidance. Best available – RSGB (rsgb.org); the exam-administration role is institutional knowledge.
