Anatomy of a UK amateur callsign

The parts of a UK amateur callsign, which characters belong in one, and who decides what is allowed. This is background for the notes shown elsewhere on this site. It is not official guidance — for that, see Ofcom. Each fact below carries its source and how confident we are in it.

How to read the sources · Authoritative = stated in an Ofcom-published document; best available = a reputable secondary source where the primary is silent; institutional knowledge = widely known but not sourced here.

the parts

The parts of a callsign

A UK amateur callsign is a short identifier for one licence, made of capital letters and digits with no spaces. It has three core parts:

prefix
the opening character(s); for the UK these begin G, M or 2.
digit
a single number.
suffix
the ending letters (for example the TEE in an M7TEE-shaped call). This is the sense of "suffix" used across this site.

A callsign may also carry a Regional Secondary Locator (RSL) — a letter inserted after the first character to show which UK nation or Crown Dependency the station is in. Ofcom defines it as "a letter, that is inserted after the initial character of the call sign … to convey the location of the Radio Equipment". Authoritative — Ofcom, Amateur Radio Wireless Telegraphy Licence Conditions Booklet (OFW611, 21 February 2024), definitions, cl. (w).

Two different meanings of "suffix". On this site and among amateurs, the suffix is the callsign's ending letters (TEE in M7TEE) — the ITU/amateur-standard sense, and the one this site always means by "suffix". Ofcom's licence T&Cs (cl. 26) use the same word, suffix, for something different: the optional addition after a slash (such as /P). To keep the two apart, this page calls that one the operating suffix (also "post-slash suffix"). The two are unrelated.
regional secondary locator

Regional Secondary Locators, and the club difference

Where an RSL is used it must be one of the letters below. There are two sets: a letter available to all licences, and — the point most often missed — an additional letter available only to a Full (Club) Licence. Authoritative — Ofcom OFW611, cl. 24 (pp 7–8).

Using an RSL is normally optional; but for an Intermediate callsign beginning with 2, an RSL must be inserted as the new second character. Ofcom may also notify a licensee of a different character to use "in place of an RSL". Authoritative — Ofcom OFW611, cl. 22, 23 and 25 (pp 7–8).

licence classes

Licence classes and their prefixes

UK amateur licences come at three classes — Foundation, Intermediate and Full — earned by successive exams; there are also Full (Club) and Full (Temporary Reciprocal) licences. Ofcom's guidance sets out the callsign formats issued at each class. Authoritative — Ofcom, Licensing guidance document for amateur radio, §5, Table 1 (Amateur Radio Call sign formats).

The class scheme has changed over the years, so older or historical records may show legacy categories or callsign formats that are not part of the current three-class scheme. Best available; the detail of past schemes is not researched here. Confidence: low.

A Full (Club) callsign is G or M followed by three letters (two by heritage). In Ofcom's table the Intermediate format is written 2#0/2#1, where the # stands for the RSL position — the second-character slot an Intermediate 2 callsign must fill. Authoritative — Ofcom guidance, Table 1; the #-as-RSL-slot reading is grounded in OFW611 cl. 25. This mirror reuses the same # notation for that slot (see the glossary's placeholder form).

which characters belong

Which characters belong in a callsign

The core callsign uses A–Z and 0–9. After the core, an operating suffix (post-slash suffix — the sense Ofcom's cl. 26 calls a "suffix", distinct from the callsign's ending-letter suffix) may be added after a forward slash /. Ofcom permits any such addition "so long as the station remains identifiable"; the familiar /P (portable), /M (mobile), /MM (maritime mobile), /A and /AM are examples, not a fixed list, and they are "not mandated by, or referenced in, the amateur radio licence". Authoritative — Ofcom OFW611, cl. 26 (p 8); Ofcom guidance, §5.8 Suffixes.

This "any addition" position is current; the post-slash suffix was previously drawn from a more limited, conventional set. Best available; the earlier history is not dug into here. Confidence: low.

The # you may see is not a letter of the callsign: it marks the RSL slot in Ofcom's format notation (as in 2#0) and in this mirror's placeholder form. The # notation is optional and style-dependent — some sources use it consistently, some omit it, and some mix the two. Our reading is that "no RSL" is the common default for Foundation and Full holders (especially in England, the largest population), whereas Intermediate callsigns require the RSL letter for everyone, so it appears consistently there. Our reading / best available — not a rigorously published rule. Confidence: low. Anything else — lower-case letters, ordinary spaces, other punctuation, or characters you cannot see — is not part of a normal callsign. Where the published data contains such characters, this site keeps the entry exactly as published and shows the canonical form beside it.

operating away or abroad

More than one slash: operating away or abroad

A callsign can carry more than one slash. Operating overseas under CEPT Recommendation T/R 61-01, a UK amateur must place "the appropriate country prefix before your call sign" (for example F/M0ABC in France) and may still add an operating suffix — so the transmitted call has both a prefix-slash and a suffix-slash. Authoritative — Ofcom guidance, §7.3 (Your call sign beyond UK jurisdiction).

The mirror image — a visitor operating in the UK under a reciprocal arrangement, shown as a UK prefix and RSL before their home callsign (for example MW/ then a non-UK callsign, optionally with /P) — appears in the register data. Best available — reciprocal/CEPT operating practice (RSGB, International Prefixes); the exact visitor format is convention, not spelled out in the primary licence documents. Confidence: medium.

who decides

Who decides what is allowed

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU)
allocates blocks of call-sign series to countries in the Radio Regulations, so a callsign's opening shows which country issued it. The UK's amateur prefixes are G, M and 2. Authoritative (instrument) — ITU Radio Regulations, Appendix 42, Table of International Call Sign Series; UK amateur prefixes per Ofcom guidance, Table 1. The full enumerated UK ranges (GAA–GZZ, MAA–MZZ, 2AA–2ZZ, and territory blocks such as VP/VS/ZB/ZN/ZQ) — best available (Wikipedia, Call signs in the United Kingdom); confidence: medium.
Ofcom
the UK regulator — issues the licences and sets the callsign rules. Authoritative — Ofcom OFW611; Ofcom Licensing guidance document for amateur radio; UK Interface Requirement 2028. See the Ofcom amateur radio licensing page.
The Radio Society of Great Britain (RSGB)
administers the amateur exams and publishes operating guidance. Best available — RSGB (rsgb.org); the exam-administration role is institutional knowledge.
