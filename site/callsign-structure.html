How a UK amateur-radio callsign is structured

A short, plain guide to the parts of a UK amateur callsign, which characters belong in one, and who decides what is allowed. It is background for the notes this site shows; it is not official guidance — for that, see Ofcom.

the parts

The parts of a callsign

A UK amateur callsign is a short code that identifies one licence. It is normally made of capital letters and digits, with no spaces. Most callsigns have three parts:

  • a prefix — the opening letter or letters (for the UK, usually G, M or 2);
  • a number — a single digit; and
  • a suffix — the final letters, normally two or three of them.

For example (illustrative), G0ABC has the prefix G, the number 0 and the suffix ABC.

Some callsigns also carry a regional letter just after the prefix, showing which UK nation the station is in — this is called the Regional Secondary Locator? (definition of the RSL in the glossary). The usual mapping is Wales W, Scotland M, Northern Ireland I, Isle of Man D, Jersey J, Guernsey U (so a Welsh station using an M callsign appears as MW…). which characters belong

Which characters belong in a callsign

A UK amateur callsign uses A–Z and 0–9. The only punctuation normally seen is the forward slash /, which separates the callsign from an added indicator — for example /P (portable), /M (mobile) or /A (an alternative address), and in visitor callsigns written as M/ followed by the visitor's home callsign. [needs verification — the list of slash indicators against a current Ofcom source.]

You may also see a # in data that comes from Ofcom. Ofcom uses it in its own notation as a stand-in for the regional-letter slot (for example the Intermediate 2#0 shape). It is a notation mark, not a letter of the callsign. [needs verification — Ofcom's use of # in callsign notation.]

Anything else — lower-case letters, spaces, other punctuation, or characters you cannot see — is not part of a normal callsign. When the published data contains such characters, this site keeps the entry exactly as published and also shows the tidied-up canonical form beside it, so nothing is hidden and nothing is silently changed.

licence levels

Licence levels and what they look like

UK amateur licences come at three levels — Foundation, Intermediate and Full — earned by passing successively harder exams. The level is reflected in the callsign's prefix and number. As a rough guide, Foundation calls have used M7, M6 and M3; Intermediate calls use 2E0/2E1 (with regional variants); and Full calls use M0, M5 and the older G series. who decides

Who decides what is allowed

Three bodies matter here:

  • The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) — a United Nations agency — divides up the opening letters of callsigns between countries in its Radio Regulations, so that a callsign's start shows which country issued it. The UK's shares include the G, M and 2 series. [needs verification — the exact ITU Radio Regulations article/appendix and the UK's allocated call-sign series blocks, with a stable ITU source URL.]
  • Ofcom — the UK's communications regulator — issues amateur licences and decides the UK callsign rules, in the amateur radio licence terms and conditions and its callsign policy. [needs verification — exact document titles and current Ofcom URLs.]
  • The Radio Society of Great Britain (RSGB) — administers the amateur exams and publishes operating and callsign guidance for licensees. [needs verification — current RSGB source URLs.]
